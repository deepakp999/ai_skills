# Patents & Intellectual Property

Part of the **product-design-development** suite (Ulrich, Eppinger & Yang, ch. 16). Usually during/after concept development and before any public disclosure; prior-art search also feeds `pdd-concept-generation`. **This is not legal advice — always tell the user to involve a qualified patent attorney/agent for filings and freedom-to-operate opinions, and that rules differ by country** (the method below uses US law as the reference point).

## IP taxonomy
| Type | Protects | Requirements | Registration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utility patent | New process, machine, article, composition, or improvement (software usually claimed as process/machine) | Useful, novel, nonobvious | Formal application |
| Design patent | Ornamental design only (like a "copyright" for the look) — limited value for most engineered goods | New ornamental design | Formal application |
| Plant patent | New plant varieties | New plant | Formal application |
| Trademark | Word, word mark, symbol identifying source | Not purely descriptive | Optional registration (™ by use; ® when registered) |
| Copyright | Original work of expression (code, docs, art) | Originality | Optional registration |
| Trade secret | Proprietary, useful information kept secret | Reasonable secrecy | Not registered |

**Patent basics**
- **Useful** (rarely a hurdle), **novel** (not publicly known in products, publications, prior patents; in the US not disclosed publicly >1 year before filing), **nonobvious** (not evident to someone of "ordinary skill in the art").
- ~2/3 of applications issue, but issued ≠ valid; few are litigated, and about half of those challenged survive.
- **Inventors** (people who substantially contributed to the claimed invention — omitting one can invalidate) vs **owner/assignee** (usually employer).
- **Offensive right**: exclude others from making, using, selling, importing — you must sue. **Defensive effect**: everything disclosed becomes prior art that blocks others' patents.
- A patent is a temporary monopoly in exchange for teaching the public the invention.

## Seven steps (inventor-drafted disclosure → attorney)
### 1. Formulate strategy and plan
- **Timing**: file **before any public disclosure** (publication, trade show, public website, test marketing) to keep foreign rights; US allows filing up to 1 year after own disclosure. Priority is **first-to-file**. Waiting longer lets you capture the features that turn out to matter, but risks someone else filing first. A public disclosure by one inventor makes it non-novel for rivals.
- **Type**:
  - **Provisional application** — description only (no formal claims), cheap (small fees), lets you mark "patent pending", preserves all options for 12 months; must fully describe everything you'll later claim; delays issuance up to a year; not examined.
  - **Regular (non-provisional) application** — examined; filing fees modest for small entities plus attorney fees.
  - **PCT application** — one filing starting international protection; defers national-phase costs ~30 months. Foreign filings can cost ~$15k per country (fees, translations, agents); the EU/EPO acts as one entity for filing.
  - Typical low-cost path: **provisional before disclosure → PCT/regular within 12 months → national filings when forced to decide**, buying 2+ years to assess commercial value.
- **Scope**: list product elements that are likely novel and nonobvious; focus on those that create real **barriers to competition**. Distinct inventions (e.g., signal processing vs paper handling) may need separate applications (the office will require division if needed). Decide the inventors.

### 2. Study prior inventions (prior art)
Why: (1) avoid infringing unexpired patents (patenting doesn't give you the right to practice); (2) gauge how broad a patent you can get; (3) learn enough to craft novel claims. Sources: product literature (current and historical), patent databases (keyword search usually suffices), technical/trade publications. **Keep a file** — known prior art must be disclosed to the patent office. Let prior art shape how the invention is described (e.g., a tube open at both ends, distinguishing it from earlier holders).

### 3. Outline claims (informally)
List every feature/characteristic you believe is unique and valuable — variants, materials, shapes, orientations, arrangements, manufacturing aspects, uses. This tells you what the description must cover.

### 4. Write the description (the "specification")
Must **teach** someone of ordinary skill to practice the invention, include the **preferred embodiment** (best mode), and persuade the examiner it is useful, different and nonobvious — a technical report that also sells the invention. Sections:
- **Title**
- **Inventors** (all)
- **Field of the invention** — what kind of device/method it relates to
- **Background** — the problem, context, what's wrong with existing solutions, why a new one is needed, advantages
- **Summary** — substance and advantages in brief
- **Brief description of drawings** — each figure in one line
- **Detailed description** — embodiments: physical structure + how it works + why features matter; then **alternative embodiments**
**Figures**: informal sketches/CAD fine for provisional; ~5–15 for a simple invention; use **reference numerals** by figure (10, 11… for Fig. 1; 20, 21… for Fig. 2), same numeral for the same feature everywhere. Write by drawing the embodiment, labeling features, describing arrangement, then function.
**Defensive disclosure**: describe every related variant you considered — even if not claimed it becomes prior art others can't patent (valuable in emerging fields). Think of *all* ways to achieve the function (a rival avoided a fluted-sleeve patent with dimples; describing dimples would have blocked or broadened it).

### 5. Refine claims
Claims define the legal right; everything else supports them.
- Structure: **"An X comprising an A, a B, and a C, wherein said A comprises a u, a v…"** — "comprising" = including but not limited to; introduce an element with "a/an", then always "said …".
- **Independent claims** stand alone; **dependent claims** ("The invention of claim N, further comprising… / wherein said…") inherit everything and add limits — they survive if the broad claim is rejected.
- **All-elements rule**: a product infringes only if it has *every* element of a claim (missing one element = no infringement), so fewer, more general elements = broader claim.
Guidelines: make claims as **general** as possible ("tubular member" not "tube"); avoid absolutes with "substantially", "approximately", "essentially"; **design around your own claim** — imagine a non-infringing competitor product, then broaden or add claims to catch it.

### 6. Pursue the application
Options: file provisional; file regular; file PCT; or defer (risk: public disclosure kills foreign rights; 1 year after disclosure kills US rights). Expect **office actions** rejecting many claims as obvious/not novel — normal; respond with arguments and amended claims; most applications eventually issue with modified claims. Use a patent attorney; DIY guides exist for tight budgets but it is administratively complex.

### 7. Reflect
- Are the essential, distinctive features in the description and claims? Is best mode described?
- What deadlines are coming (keep a docket)?
- What went well/poorly in drafting?
- What did prior art teach — technologies to license, competitor patents expiring?
- How strong is the position — a real barrier, or only a deterrent to direct copies?
- Did we start too early/late?

## Trademarks
Brands/product/company names; prevent confusing, unfair competition. Must not be purely descriptive (suggestive coined names work). Use ™ with use in commerce; register for ® at modest cost. Pick names whose **exact domain names** are available. Mark competitors' trademarks when used in comparisons.

## Advice for individual inventors
- A patent supports commercialization but isn't central; patenting can usually wait until key technical and market risks are addressed.
- A patent alone (and an idea even more so) rarely has commercial value; value comes from a resolved design — a working, tested prototype makes a patent worth more.
- Licensing to big manufacturers as an individual is very hard; be ready to commercialize yourself or with a small partner; prove the market first.
- File a **provisional** cheaply to buy a year while you evaluate.

## Digital / AI adaptation
Software and AI inventions are usually claimed as methods/systems; eligibility rules for software vary by country — flag this for counsel. Often trade secrets (models, data, prompts, pipelines), copyright (code), contracts/licensing and data rights matter more than patents; check open-source licence obligations and dataset/model licences as part of "prior art and freedom to operate".
